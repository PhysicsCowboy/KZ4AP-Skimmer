# Race a small LSTM against a duration HMM

The decoder to build and benchmark first is not one algorithm but a pair on a shared test bench. Start with a cheap fix to the current baseline: filter to the element bandwidth *before* the envelope detector and make its outputs soft. Then run two candidates head to head. The first is a small streaming CNN+LSTM network trained with CTC (connectionist temporal classification), following VE3NEA's open-source DeepCW recipe. The second is a Bell-style explicit-duration hidden Markov model (HMM) decoder with a Kalman-tracked amplitude. The only controlled comparison in the whole literature favors the neural option. It is VE3NEA's own 2024 test of a roughly 375,000-parameter CNN+LSTM against his closed-source CW Skimmer, run on identical synthetic data. The network matched Skimmer near threshold and beat it clearly under fast fading and hand keying. At 1 Hz Doppler spread, +17.8 dB key-on SNR in 500 Hz, and paddle keying, Skimmer's character error rate (CER) was 11.1% and the network's was 3.5%. The HMM path has weaker evidence: AG1LE's port of Bell's 1977 decoder showed a roughly 3% CER floor and was unstable. It still deserves a slot because it has an explicit timing model, costs little per channel, and is the natural back end for a hybrid in which a network supplies soft key-on probabilities. Every published sensitivity figure for steady (non-fading) noise, once converted to this project's convention, lands within a few dB of 0 dB key-on SNR in 500 Hz. The differences among them are smaller than the uncertainty in how each author defined SNR. So published headline numbers cannot pick the winner. Only the project's own benchmark, with fading, poor fists, speed changes and interference (QRM), can.

**Evidence labels.** Each claim carries one of three labels. **[fact]** means documented by the primary source: the author's code, manual, thesis or blog. **[secondhand]** means reported by a third party, or taken from a summary of a source that was not read line by line. **[inference]** means reasoning by the research team or by this report. Tables abbreviate these as F, S and I. No claim here was independently reproduced unless it says "measured."

**Symbols.** All SNRs are power ratios in dB.

| Symbol | Meaning | Units |
|---|---|---|
| S_B | SNR with the noise measured in bandwidth B | dB |
| S₅₀₀ | the project convention, key-on (key-down) carrier power over noise power in a 500 Hz bandwidth; the same convention as CW Skimmer and the Reverse Beacon Network (RBN) | dB |
| d | keying duty cycle, the fraction of time the key is down (about 0.5 for text) | dimensionless |
| T | dit duration, 1.2/WPM (PARIS timing) | s |
| WPM | keying speed | words per minute |
| E_s/N₀ | energy in one key-down dit divided by the noise power spectral density | dB |
| f_D | Doppler spread of a Rayleigh-fading channel; larger values mean faster fading (QSB) | Hz |
| CER | character error rate: the Levenshtein edit distance (insertions, deletions and substitutions) divided by the reference length | dimensionless |
| r | the decoder's input sample rate, 1500 samples/s complex | samples/s |
| MAC | one multiply-accumulate operation | — |

## One SNR yardstick shrinks the published gaps to a few dB

Morse SNR figures in the literature use at least five noise bandwidths (2000, 2500, 3000 and 500 Hz, plus "the signal bandwidth"). They also use two definitions of signal power: key-on power, and power averaged over keying. For white noise and a signal lying inside both bandwidths, the conversion to this project's convention is S₅₀₀ = S_B + 10·log₁₀(B / 500 Hz) + K. Here K = 0 dB for key-on definitions, and K = −10·log₁₀(d) ≈ +3.0 dB for power averaged over keying at d ≈ 0.5 [inference]. The project already adopts key-down power over noise in 500 Hz for its synthetic recordings. Its channel filter has a noise bandwidth of 252 Hz, so SNR in the channel is S₅₀₀ + 3.0 dB ([signal-processing.md](../signal-processing.md)) [fact]. CW Skimmer uses the same convention for spots. VE3NEA's own description is that the signal is taken through a 50 Hz filter with key-off and transition samples discarded, and its power is estimated under a Rayleigh fading model. The noise density is taken from the flat part of the spectrum across the whole receiver bandwidth and multiplied by 500 Hz ([RBN blog, VE3NEA email, 2014](http://reversebeacon.blogspot.com/2014/03/understanding-signal-to-noise-ratio-snr.html)) [fact]. The manual calls it "the key-on signal-to-noise ratio in the 500 Hz bandwidth averaged over QSB" ([CW Skimmer manual](http://dxatlas.com/CWSkimmer/Files/CwSkimmer.pdf)) [fact]. One difference remains. The project's S₅₀₀ is a generator setting, known exactly, while RBN's is an estimate built up over about 45 s. Because RBN's noise floor is band-wide rather than local, its SNR reads high in crowded or splattered segments ([RBN blog 2014](http://reversebeacon.blogspot.com/2014/03/understanding-signal-to-noise-ratio-snr.html)) [secondhand for the 45 s figure; inference for the bias].

A second yardstick links SNR to detection theory. For a key-on SNR S (linear) in bandwidth B, the dit energy over noise density is E_s/N₀ = S·B·T [inference: definitional algebra]. At 25 WPM (T = 48 ms), **S₅₀₀ = 0 dB means E_s/N₀ ≈ 13.8 dB per dit**. The same S₅₀₀ gives 11.8 dB at 40 WPM and 17.0 dB at 12 WPM. A typical RBN spot of about +5 dB (the manual's examples show 4–7 dB) therefore carries roughly 19 dB of energy per dit at 25 WPM. That is far from any detection limit, so the competition between decoders is fought at S₅₀₀ ≈ 0 dB and below, and under fading and bad timing [inference]. The research notes add, from unchecked textbook memory, that noncoherent on-off keying needs something like 12–14 dB E_s/N₀ for a symbol error rate near 10⁻³. That figure should be confirmed in Proakis or Van Trees before anyone relies on it [inference].

Read side by side, the steady-noise entries cluster tightly. fldigi's matched-filter slicer reaches 1.2% CER at S₅₀₀ between −2.2 and +0.8 dB, depending on PathSim's undocumented definition. e04's roughly 3.6-million-parameter network reports under 8% at 0 dB and under 1.5% at +2 dB. Bell's pre-filtered simulation reaches about 10% letter error near −1 dB. These spreads of 2–3 dB are the same size as the definitional ambiguity. They are also confounded by speed, which moves E_s/N₀ by several dB at fixed S₅₀₀. **No published AWGN curve demonstrates that any decoder is more than about 3 dB better than a well-filtered hard-decision slicer on machine-timed code** [inference]. The large, well-measured differences appear only in VE3NEA's fading and hand-keying grid. That is where the project's benchmark must look.

## CW Skimmer is a closed Bayesian target with documented spotting rules

Afreet's only published description of the decoder is one phrase: "a very sensitive CW decoding algorithm based on the methods of Bayesian statistics" ([manual](http://dxatlas.com/CWSkimmer/Files/CwSkimmer.pdf); [product page](https://www.dxatlas.com/CwSkimmer/)) [fact]. The single technical hint is a private email VE3NEA sent to AG1LE. It advised expressing prior knowledge as probabilities and updating them with data: compute the probability that the signal is present rather than deciding at every sample, and carry probabilities "all the way to the word recognition unit." In that email VE3NEA also said he spent about eight years and tried hundreds of algorithms ([AG1LE, Jan 2013](http://ag1le.blogspot.com/2013/01/towards-bayesian-morse-decoder.html)) [fact, relayed]. Circumstantial evidence fits a soft-decision pipeline. Low-confidence characters print in pale colors. A release note cites improved "word segmentation." The SNR estimator classifies samples as key-on, key-off or transition ([manual](http://dxatlas.com/CWSkimmer/Files/CwSkimmer.pdf); [RBN 2014](http://reversebeacon.blogspot.com/2014/03/understanding-signal-to-noise-ratio-snr.html)) [fact]. That the internals resemble an HMM or particle filter is an informed guess, not a documented fact [inference]. Whether a dictionary prior is used inside the decoder was speculated by AG1LE and a commenter but never confirmed ([AG1LE 2013](http://ag1le.blogspot.com/2013/01/towards-bayesian-morse-decoder.html)) [secondhand].

The architecture is better documented than the algorithm. Skimmer creates one decoder per CW signal found in the passband, with up to **700 parallel decoders on a 3 GHz Pentium 4** and an "Adaptive" decoder count that follows available CPU ([manual](http://dxatlas.com/CWSkimmer/Files/CwSkimmer.pdf)) [fact]. RBN administrators describe each decoder as about 50 Hz wide with a 256-character history ([RBN "How to get spotted"](https://www.reversebeacon.net/pages/How+to+get+spotted+by+the+RBN+44)) [secondhand]. The 50 Hz width agrees with the 50 Hz extraction filter VE3NEA describes for SNR [fact]. Dividing 3×10⁹ cycles/s among 700 decoders bounds the whole per-decoder budget, shared overhead included, at about **4.3 million CPU cycles per second of signal** on a 2000s-era CPU. That is the best available existence proof that a probabilistic decoder can be cheap enough for hundreds of channels [inference from a vendor claim].

Spotting is a separate, mostly rule-based layer. A message analyzer extracts callsigns and keywords (CQ, DE, TEST, later also FD, SS, NA and UP). A call must then pass pattern validation against Skimmer Server's patt3ch.lst file, whose patterns use @ for a letter and # for a digit, and repeat enough times within the 256-character buffer ([RBN 2021](http://reversebeacon.blogspot.com/2021/09/how-skimmer-server-decides-what-to-spot.html); [N6TV CTU 2018](https://www.contestuniversity.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/4.-N6TV-CW-and-RTTY-Skimmers-and-the-Reverse-Beacon-Network.pdf)) [secondhand, from discussion with VE3NEA]. The repeat thresholds are:

| Validation level | Watch list | Common ("+") pattern | Listed pattern | Unlisted pattern |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimal | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Normal | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Aggressive | 2 | 4 | 4 | not spotted |
| Paranoid | 2 | not spotted | not spotted | not spotted |

Source: [RBN 2021](http://reversebeacon.blogspot.com/2021/09/how-skimmer-server-decides-what-to-spot.html) [S]. A simpler RBN page gives 2/3/4 repeats, which differs on the unlisted count [S].

Skimmer Server does not use the MASTER.DTA super-check-partial file, although standalone Skimmer does. It will not re-spot the same call near the same frequency within 10 minutes. Busted calls are caught mostly downstream, by CT1BOH's unpublished cross-skimmer statistics ([N6TV CTU 2018](https://www.contestuniversity.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/4.-N6TV-CW-and-RTTY-Skimmers-and-the-Reverse-Beacon-Network.pdf); [RBN tutorial 2013](http://reversebeacon.blogspot.com/2013/12/a-new-tutorial-on-using-rbn.html)) [secondhand]. The table behaves like a crude posterior threshold: prior plausibility is traded against the number of independent decodes. A decoder with calibrated soft scores could replace it with an explicit callsign posterior [inference].

Skimmer's known weaknesses are timing-related. Sender guidance asks for constant speed within a message, 3:1 weighting and clean spacing without half-spaces next to the call ([RBN 2021](http://reversebeacon.blogspot.com/2021/09/how-skimmer-server-decides-what-to-spot.html); [RBN page](https://www.reversebeacon.net/pages/How+to+get+spotted+by+the+RBN+44)) [secondhand]. G4ILO reportedly found it excellent on weak signals but worse than MRP40 at word spacing ([G4ILO blog](http://blog.g4ilo.com/2012/12/best-morse-decoder.html)) [secondhand; unverified, page did not load]. No controlled CER-versus-SNR benchmark of Skimmer exists except VE3NEA's own 2024 comparison, discussed below.

## Hard decisions throw away what pre-detection filtering could keep

The project's baseline belongs to the oldest family of Morse decoders. Gold's MAUDE (MIT Lincoln Laboratory, 1959) and Guenther's PDP-12 program (Air Force Institute of Technology, 1973) kept running averages of mark and space lengths and classified durations against them. Guenther's rules were: a mark longer than the average is a dash, a mark shorter than half a dot is discarded as noise, and runs of characters with no gap are re-split at the largest internal space ([IEEE Xplore, Gold](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/1057478/); [Guenther transcription](http://alanpich.github.io/Morse-Code-Recognition/computer-recognition-program/program-description/code-translation-section.html)) [fact for Guenther]. MAUDE reportedly decoded 90–95% of 184 operators, under unstated conditions ([IEEE abstract](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/1057478/)) [secondhand]. fldigi is the modern version. Its chain is:

1. An FFT low-pass filter, optionally "matched" at 5×WPM/1.2 Hz.
2. A moving-average envelope about half a dot long.
3. Normalization by an automatic gain control (AGC) and a noise-floor tracker, with decay constants of roughly 200 ms up and 1000 ms down.
4. Upper and lower hysteresis thresholds.
5. A speed tracker fed only by adjacent dot–dash pairs whose length ratio is between 2 and 4.
6. A single dit/dah split, followed by table lookup.

AG1LE's self-organizing-map (SOM) codebook match is an optional replacement for the table lookup ([fldigi cw.cxx](https://github.com/w1hkj/fldigi/blob/master/src/cw_rtty/cw.cxx)) [fact]. Its measured cliff runs from 1.2% CER to 50.7% over just 5 dB ([AG1LE 2013](http://ag1le.blogspot.com/2013/01/morse-decoder-snr-vs-cer-testing.html)) [fact]. That is the signature of one flipped or split element corrupting a whole character [inference]. The SOM always returns *some* character and printed "garbage" in noise ([AG1LE 2012](http://ag1le.blogspot.com/2012/05/fldigi-more-test-results-on.html)) [fact]. ggmorse fits speed and threshold by exhaustive search over a window, which assumes one speed and one threshold per window ([ggmorse source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ggerganov/ggmorse/master/src/ggmorse.cpp)) [fact; the consequence is inference]. PA3FWM's RSCW performs a Viterbi-like maximum-likelihood search, which its author calls optimal for on-off keying in AWGN. It works only with machine-perfect timing and publishes no SNR figures ([RSCW](https://www.pa3fwm.nl/software/rscw/algorithm.html)) [fact].

E. L. Bell's work at the Naval Postgraduate School is the central classical reference. It is also the clearest evidence on **filtering before versus after the envelope detector**. In his 1975 thesis, post-detection smoothing on a 2 kHz input needed about +6 dB (2 kHz) for about 10% letter error. Adding a 100 Hz band-pass *before* detection moved that point to about −7 dB. A pre-detection Kalman filter tracking the in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) pair gave about 1% bit error at −14 dB ([Bell 1975](https://archive.org/stream/processingofmanu00bellpdf/processingofmanu00bell_djvu.txt)) [fact]. Detection theory explains the direction of this. With no signal the envelope is Rayleigh-distributed, and with a signal present it is Rician. At low input SNR the noise×noise term dominates a square-law or envelope detector's output and suppresses the weak signal, so narrowing the bandwidth ahead of the detector reduces that loss ([Univ. of Michigan EECS 555](http://www.eecs.umich.edu/courses/eecs555/chap5.pdf)) [secondhand for the distributions; inference for the mechanism]. Bell's 13 dB shift came from narrowing a 2 kHz input by a factor of 20, where the detector input sat far below 0 dB. The project's detector already sees only 252 Hz, where the input SNR is S₅₀₀ + 3 dB. A dit-matched pre-filter of about 1/T ≈ 21 Hz at 25 WPM would narrow the noise by a further 10·log₁₀(252/21) ≈ 10.8 dB at the detector input. The realized CER gain will be smaller than that, because the baseline's post-detection smoothing (a first-order filter with τ = ¼ dit) already recovers part of it. **The gain should be measured, not assumed** ([signal-processing.md](../signal-processing.md)) [inference]. Bell also found that the Viterbi code model helped only when the bit error was already below about 3%. At 35 WPM and +6 dB (2 kHz) it cut bit error from 0.83% to 0.35%, and at +4 dB it gave nothing ([Bell 1975](https://archive.org/stream/processingofmanu00bellpdf/processingofmanu00bell_djvu.txt)) [fact]. **Most of the sensitivity comes from the soft front end, not from the sequence model** [inference].

Bell's 1977 dissertation generalized this into optimal Bayesian estimation. The keying process is a memory-conditioned Markov process with keystate, position within the letter, letter context, and a speed "control" variable. It is observed through a Gauss–Markov fading amplitude. The optimal decoder is "a denumerable but exponentially expanding set of linear Kalman filters operating on a dynamically evolving trellis," and it was tested only on random letters ([Internet Archive abstract](https://archive.org/details/optimalbayesiane00bell)) [fact; model details secondhand from an OCR summary]. AG1LE hand-entered Bell's Fortran into about 3,335 lines of C with Bell's verbal permission. It has six keystates, per-path Kalman likelihoods, Bayesian path updates and an 8–80 WPM speed tracker ([AG1LE Part 1](http://ag1le.blogspot.com/2013/09/new-morse-decoder-part-1.html); [Part 2](http://ag1le.blogspot.com/2013/12/new-morse-decoder-part-2.html)) [fact]. The results were mixed. He reported "significant accuracy improvement" over fldigi on real noisy signals. He also recorded an unexplained roughly 3% CER floor, a noise estimator that did not track, intermittent overflows, and a speed estimator that could stick at its limits. A 2014 multi-channel build decoded nine signals but lost accuracy when several stations at different speeds were present ([Part 2](http://ag1le.blogspot.com/2013/12/new-morse-decoder-part-2.html); [Part 6](http://ag1le.blogspot.com/2014/07/new-morse-decoder-part-6.html)) [fact]. The failure modes point to parameters and implementation, not to the model [inference]. They are nonetheless the engineering risk a Bell-style decoder carries. Its cost is roughly paths × successors × a scalar or 2×2 Kalman update per sample. For example, 100 surviving paths × 6 successors × about 20 floating-point operations at a decimated 200 samples/s is about 2.4 MFLOP/s per channel. That fits inside Skimmer's inferred budget, but it is an estimate, not a measurement [inference]. No Morse-specific particle filter has been published. A Rao–Blackwellized particle filter with continuous speed and amplitude integrated out by a Kalman filter is untested design territory [inference].

## Small streaming networks win where timing and fading break the model

Neural Morse decoders have converged on an automatic-speech-recognition pattern: a spectrogram, a small convolutional front end, a recurrent or Conformer encoder, and CTC training ([VE3NEA/DeepCW](https://github.com/VE3NEA/DeepCW); [AG1LE 2020](https://ag1le.blogspot.com/2020/04/new-real-time-deep-learning-morse.html); [morseformer](https://github.com/sderhy/morseformer)) [fact]. The most important entry is VE3NEA's DeepCW (October 2024, MIT license). It has two 3×3 convolutions with pooling, a Dense(64) layer and a **stateful LSTM(256)** feeding a softmax over 43 classes. That comes to **about 375,000 parameters**, and the model is rebuilt for batch-1 streaming with greedy CTC decoding. Its input is a 22-bin crop at 11.7 Hz spacing (about 258 Hz) at 93.75 frames/s ([VE3NEA/DeepCW model.ipynb](https://github.com/VE3NEA/DeepCW)) [fact]. The geometry maps exactly onto this project's channel. At r = 1500 samples/s, a 128-point FFT gives 1500/128 = 11.72 Hz bins, and a 16-sample hop gives 93.75 frames/s. The notebook's 50 ms window becomes 75 samples, zero-padded [inference]. Its training generator is the most principled found:

- **Text:** character and word statistics drawn from CW Skimmer decodes of real band traffic.
- **Fading:** Rayleigh fading with f_D = 0.1–3 Hz.
- **SNR and speed:** SNR from −16 to +50 dB (key-on, 3 kHz) and speed from 8 to 50 WPM.
- **Keying:** log-normal element timing for each keying style, with a mix of 25% hand key, 50% paddle and 25% computer.
- **Transmitter faults:** a random key-on/off imbalance.

Source: [VE3NEA/DeepCW data_generation.ipynb](https://github.com/VE3NEA/DeepCW) [fact]. Training ran at 80–100 ms per batch on a GPU. With 329 batches per epoch over 50 epochs, that implies under half an hour of GPU time [inference from documented figures]. The research notes do not record whether pretrained weights ship with the repository.

VE3NEA benchmarked it against CW Skimmer on about 30,000 characters per point ([accuracy_charts.ipynb](https://github.com/VE3NEA/DeepCW)) [fact]. SNRs are converted to S₅₀₀ by adding 7.8 dB [inference]. Each cell gives CER as Skimmer / DeepCW:

| Condition (24 WPM unless noted) | S₅₀₀ +1.8 dB | +7.8 dB | +13.8 dB | +17.8 dB | +27.8 dB |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paddle, f_D = 0.1 Hz | 0.364 / 0.373 | 0.101 / 0.137 | 0.039 / 0.048 | 0.022 / 0.025 | 0.011 / 0.009 |
| Paddle, f_D = 1 Hz | 0.457 / 0.452 | 0.221 / 0.195 | 0.135 / 0.074 | 0.111 / 0.035 | 0.101 / 0.009 |
| Paddle, f_D = 3 Hz | 0.654 / 0.533 | 0.459 / 0.241 | 0.419 / 0.085 | 0.396 / 0.039 | 0.390 / 0.010 |
| Hand key, f_D = 0.1 Hz | 0.429 / 0.412 | 0.188 / 0.186 | 0.106 / 0.110 | 0.091 / 0.082 | 0.076 / 0.069 |
| Hand key, f_D = 3 Hz | 0.790 / 0.586 | 0.633 / 0.304 | 0.566 / 0.147 | 0.550 / 0.098 | 0.533 / 0.066 |

Three patterns stand out [inference]. First, under slow fading the two are equal to within a few percent, and **the network is worse at 12 WPM**. The small network buys no raw sensitivity. Second, Skimmer's error rate stays near 40% at 3 Hz Doppler spread even at +27.8 dB. That is a model-mismatch floor, not a noise floor, and the network removes most of it. Third, hand keying leaves both decoders with a 6–8% floor at high SNR, so timing variability remains unsolved. One caution: this is the network's author running his own generator. The test data share a distribution with the training data, and no real recordings were scored. The notes also raise the possibility that VE3NEA's CER excludes spaces, which would hide the segmentation errors that dominate real-audio failures [inference].

The other neural entries sharpen that caution. e04's DeepCW is an ONNX model of about 3.6 million parameters. Its operators, with self-attention and no LSTM, suggest a Conformer-like encoder ([deepcw-engine](https://github.com/e04/deepcw-engine)) [measured by the research team from the ONNX graph; the architecture label is inference]. Its README claims the table figures in AWGN only, with an unpublished training recipe. On three YouTube QSO clips it made fewer character errors than CW Skimmer, fldigi and ggmorse, mostly failing on run-together words, and the author disclaims this as a general ranking ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/e04/web-deep-cw-decoder/main/README.md)) [fact]. morseformer (April–September 2026, about 3.9 million parameters, Conformer with CTC and RNN-T heads plus an optional character 3-gram model) reports **17.75% CER on 31 minutes of real hand-keyed ragchews** even after a real-audio fine-tune. That breaks down as 8.45% on one operator set and 28.6% on a held-out operator. A GPT-style language model was switched off because "it hurt amateur jargon" ([morseformer](https://github.com/sderhy/morseformer)) [fact]. AG1LE's 2020 CNN-LSTM-CTC misread characters that straddled frame boundaries, confused numbers, and learned no word spaces ([AG1LE 2020](https://ag1le.blogspot.com/2020/04/new-real-time-deep-learning-morse.html)) [fact]. Across every real-audio report, **the dominant failure is spacing and segmentation on hand-sent code, not tone detection** [inference].

Cost separates the neural options sharply. VE3NEA's model is estimated at **15–20 million MAC/s per channel**, from its LSTM (about 328,000 MACs per step at about 23 steps/s) and its second convolution. That is about 5–6 GMAC/s for 300 channels, feasible on one desktop core with SIMD. As a single [N × 320] × [320 × 1024] matrix multiply per step, it batches naturally across channels. A Raspberry Pi likely handles only a fraction of that; the number is unmeasured [inference]. The research team timed e04's ONNX model on one thread of an Alder Lake laptop CPU. A 10 s window took 97–474 ms (21–102× real time), and a 30 s window took about 3.2 s. The superlinear growth is consistent with attention. Run as a sliding window refreshed every second, this implies only about 2–10 channels per core [measured, single noisy run with random input; per-channel figure is inference]. No source publishes per-channel throughput, latency, or Raspberry Pi results for any neural Morse decoder. Licensing also matters for adoption. VE3NEA's DeepCW is MIT, morseformer is Apache-2.0, and e04's engine is AGPL-3.0 [fact]. The license of AG1LE's morse-wip Bell port was not established [gap].

## Rank the candidates by evidence per CPU cycle

The comparison below condenses the evidence. "Weak signals" gives the best defensible S₅₀₀ reading and inherits every conversion caveat above.

The ranked recommendation follows from three facts. The largest demonstrated gains are in fading and timing robustness. Sensitivity in steady noise is set mostly by the front end. And CPU across hundreds of channels, especially on a Pi, is a hard constraint.

**1. Soft, pre-filtered front end on the existing baseline (build first, cheapest).** Add a second-stage complex filter matched to the current dit estimate, a few tens of Hz wide, at 1500 samples/s. This is the "two-stage channel filtering" already on the backlog. Convert the envelope into a per-sample log-likelihood ratio from Rician-versus-Rayleigh densities, using the tracked mark level and noise floor. Bell's pre-versus-post detection results and fldigi's matched-filter option are the evidence. Cost is a few dozen operations per sample. The soft output is exactly the emission stream that options 3 and 4 consume, so no work is wasted [inference].

**2. Small streaming CNN+LSTM-CTC, VE3NEA recipe (the primary challenger).** Reimplement VE3NEA's MIT-licensed model, or retrain it on the project's own port of his generator, using a 128-point FFT with a 16-sample hop on the channel stream. Batch the LSTM step across all active channels and export to ONNX, with int8 quantization as a later option. It has the only controlled evidence of beating a strong Bayesian decoder, and its cost is known to within a factor of about 2. Risks: the gap between synthetic training data and real audio (morseformer), spacing on hand-sent code, worse performance than Skimmer at slow speed, and Raspberry Pi throughput [inference].

**3. Explicit-duration HMM with beam search (the classical challenger).** The state is keystate/element (about 6 classes) × time in state in dit units × a discrete speed grid. The per-channel or per-path Kalman amplitude and noise trackers follow Bell 1977, fed by option 1's likelihoods. Evidence is weaker: Bell's simulations, AG1LE's unstable port, and Skimmer's unpublished success. It earns third place because its costs are low and predictable, and its explicit timing model targets the spacing failures that dominate neural real-audio errors. It also yields calibrated character probabilities for a callsign posterior [inference].

**4. Hybrids, once 2 and 3 exist.** Three designs are worth testing. The first feeds the network's per-frame key-on posteriors into option 3's HMM as its emission probabilities, keeping the HMM's timing model while gaining the network's fading robustness. The second runs CTC beam search with a ham-specific character model: callsign patterns in the spirit of patt3ch.lst, plus CQ/TEST/DE/5NN/TU exchange templates. The third arbitrates: the cheap decoder runs everywhere, and the network runs only on channels with low confidence or detected QSB, capping CPU. The research found no quantitative result for any neural-plus-classical fusion in Morse. The closest published systems pair a neural recognizer with an n-gram word splitter ([morseformer](https://github.com/sderhy/morseformer)), or ship a neural and a "traditional Bayesian" decoder side by side without fusing them ([CW-LAB](https://github.com/lucpaysan/CW-LAB)) [fact; the three designs are inference]. Language priors must raise confidence without inventing calls. morseformer's experience with its GPT model is the warning [fact/inference].

**Not first:** e04-class and morseformer-class attention models (about 10× the parameters of option 2, non-streaming or PyTorch-bound, 800 Hz audio input, and e04 is AGPL). Also not first: ggmorse, RSCW and SOM matching, whose assumptions (one steady speed, perfect timing, forced nearest match) contradict the skimmer's traffic [inference].

The benchmark should reproduce VE3NEA's grid so that his numbers become an external anchor, then add what no source has measured. Every scenario reports CER with spaces scored separately from characters, S₅₀₀ key-on as generated, WPM, and CPU time per channel-second on a desktop and on a Pi 5:

## Conclusion

The evidence overturns a tempting assumption: that the path past a hard-decision decoder is sensitivity, with neural networks as the obvious upgrade. Normalized to key-on SNR in 500 Hz, no published result separates decoders by more than the ambiguity in their SNR definitions in steady noise. The one controlled test shows the small network's advantage is robustness to fast fading and imperfect keying, not a lower threshold. Meanwhile Bell's half-century-old experiments locate most of the recoverable sensitivity in the front end, before any sequence model runs. For this project that means the first measurable win is probably the pre-detection filter and soft likelihoods. The real contest is then between two different ways of modeling the channel and the operator, one learned and one explicit.

The open problems are the ones nobody has measured: interference within tens of Hz, abrupt speed changes, real-audio spacing, and throughput on a Raspberry Pi at hundreds of channels. The project's benchmark would be the first public source for all of them. That makes the generator and scoring harness, with the SNR convention written into every result, a more durable contribution than any single decoder. Suppose the hybrid, with network emissions feeding a duration HMM, outperforms both parents. It would then be the first published evidence that the probabilistic pipeline VE3NEA described in 2013 and the neural recognizer he released in 2024 are complements rather than rivals [inference].
