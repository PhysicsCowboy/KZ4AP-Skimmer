## 2. Method, data, evaluation, results

### 2.1 What MAUDE is (hardware)

MAUDE (Morse AUtomatic DEcoder) is a **transistorized special-purpose digital computer**, not a general-purpose machine: "transistorized flip-flops as the basic storage unit" [fact, p. 20]. It takes a hand-sent Morse signal as input and prints decoded text on a teletypewriter [fact, p. 17]. A block diagram (Fig. 5, p. 20) shows: Morse code input → logger (stores logarithms of alternate mark and space durations digitally) → mark/space storage registers → an error-sign detector → rule blocks (M6, L6&S6, S3) feeding "bookkeeping" blocks that hold the running-average thresholds → an alphabet test → teletype output [fact, p. 20]. A photo of the lab model (a full instrument rack) appears as Fig. 7, p. 23 [fact]. Two companion papers are referenced as "in preparation": one on the engineering/hardware design, one a mathematical/statistical treatment of the rules L6, S6, M6 (the latter is also sketched in this paper's Appendix, based on a theorem of L. C. Maximon) [fact, p. 17, p. 23]. **The paper never mentions a radio receiver, envelope/AM detector, AGC, or carrier** — the input is treated as an already-binary keying signal (mark/space durations), consistent with a landline or direct hand-key source, not an RF front end [inference from absence, p. 17–24].

One numerical experiment (the fixed-vs-variable-Δ comparison, Table IV) was run **off-line on an IBM 704** as a simulation, separate from the transistorized hardware [fact, p. 23].

### 2.2 Decoding method: fixed (structural) rules first, then adaptive thresholds

This is the key structural point the survey currently compresses to "classified durations against running averages," which is only true of the *second* stage:

**Stage 1 — fixed rules, based on Morse-code structure and crude language statistics, need no threshold at all** [fact, pp. 18–19]. Applied over sliding windows of *6* successive marks or *6* successive spaces (or smaller windows of 3–5 for one variant):
- **L6**: the longest of 6 successive spaces is (almost always) a long space (character/word space).
- **S6**: the shortest of 6 successive spaces is (almost always) a short space (symbol space).
- **S3**: the shortest of 3 successive spaces from the set already flagged long by L6 is usually a character space (splits long spaces into character vs. word spaces).
- **Lk (the "alphabet test"), k = 3, 4, or 5**: the longest of k successive spaces is almost always a long space *if* the marks it separates do not themselves form a valid Morse character — i.e., it uses the fact that the Morse alphabet contains all 1-, 2-, and 3-mark combinations but only a minority of the possible 4-, 5-, and 6-mark combinations, and none longer than 6 marks (with one exception: the error sign, see §2.5) [fact, p. 18].
- **M6**: the shortest of 6 successive marks is usually a dot.

Gold justifies L6/Lk purely from the combinatorics of the Morse alphabet (code structure), and justifies S6 from a genuine language argument: "five or more successive one-symbol characters (E or T) rarely occur in English," versus a probability of (1/13)^5 for random letter-cipher text [fact, p. 19]. This — the S6 justification — is the concrete referent of the abstract's "certain elementary properties of language," not a semantic or vocabulary model; Gold is explicit that MAUDE does *not* attempt word- or meaning-level decoding (§III, p. 18), and the abstract says performance will stay "inferior to that of a man until more sophisticated language rules, using at least a word vocabulary, are included" [fact, p. 17].

Consistent with this being a thin language contribution: Table III (p. 22) shows essentially no difference in MAUDE's character error rate between English text, number-cipher text, and letter-cipher text (composite 1.12% English, 72/6438; 0.99% letter cipher, 106/10772; 1% number cipher, 71/7088) [fact, p. 22, column headers checked against the rendered page image] — i.e., across this test the S6 language heuristic did not measurably separate "real English" performance from random-text performance. **[inference]** This suggests the accuracy of MAUDE in this test was carried almost entirely by the structural/timing rules, not by language knowledge, which undercuts a naive reading of "elementary properties of language" as a meaningful accuracy driver.

**Stage 2 — adaptive thresholds on whatever Stage 1 left unclassified** [fact, p. 19]. This is the part that matches "running averages of mark and space lengths":
- Elements "tagged" (confidently classified) by the Stage-1 rules feed exponentially weighted running averages θ_M, θ_S, θ_L of mark, short-space, and long-space (log-)duration, updated by θ(n) = θ(n−1) + (1/β)[x(n) − θ(n−1)], with β = 4 (a weighting/time-constant parameter, not a threshold) [fact, eq. 1, p. 19]. Working in **log duration** is explicit and deliberate — "less inherent fluctuation than differences" and increased dynamic range (footnote 3, p. 19) — this is a log-domain matched/adaptive threshold, not a linear one.
- Thresholds are then set as T = θ + Δ (e.g., T_S = θ_S + Δ_S), where the Δ's are "empirically determined constants" (Fig. 3, p. 20). Two schemes were compared (§V, §IX): (a) Δ fixed globally from ~10 analyzed messages, (b) Δ chosen per operator and then held fixed for that operator's whole message. A later experiment generalized this further to a **variable-Δ scheme, recomputed for every 100 samples** of marks/spaces, run as an IBM-704 simulation [fact, p. 23].
- The remaining ambiguous marks are classified only by the mark threshold T_M = θ_M + Δ_M (fixed Δ_M in the built hardware); remaining spaces by L6/S6 followed by a threshold test at T_S (§VII, p. 20).
- A **speed/fist correction**: Smith-Vaniz's private-communication observation that spaces following dashes run short relative to spaces following dots motivated a transformed space duration S′ = S + αM (M = preceding mark duration), with **α = 0.18** found broadly useful for hand-sent Morse; Gold notes this transform is "more of a hindrance than a help" for bug-sent Morse, though not harmful enough to noticeably hurt MAUDE's performance there [fact, p. 19]. This is MAUDE's one explicit accommodation for different *sending instruments* (hand key vs. semiautomatic "bug," §II, p. 17) as opposed to different *operators*.

**How this tracks speed changes / "poor fists":** the running averages are recomputed continuously through a message, so they "should accurately mirror the properties (such as word speed) of the particular message" (p. 19) — i.e., MAUDE re-centers on the current sender's speed within a message rather than assuming a fixed WPM. Handling of *inter-operator* variability (the paper repeatedly calls this "the main problem," p. 18) is the whole point of the adaptive-Δ design; Gold reports the variable-Δ (updated every 100 samples) scheme meaningfully outperforms even a per-operator-fixed Δ (see Table IV below), i.e., adapting faster within a message helps.

### 2.3 The error-sign detector

A separate, simple detector: if 5+ successive log-domain differences between adjacent marks (and between adjacent spaces) are each below a fixed threshold (a ratio of 2:1, i.e., log-difference of 10 in Gold's units), an error sign (6–20 dots, or "IMI"/"II") is flagged and used to inhibit/reset the rest of the system so a long run of dots doesn't corrupt the running averages [fact, p. 20].

### 2.4 The test: 184 *messages*, 53 operators — not "184 operators"

This is a direct correction to the project's existing text (see §3 below). Gold's abstract states MAUDE "successfully decoded between 90 per cent and 95 per cent of 184 operators" [fact, p. 17, verified against the rendered page image] — but the body of the paper (§IX, p. 22) defines the actual test corpus as **184 messages, sent by 53 different operators, containing about 45,000 characters total** [fact, p. 22]. The abstract's wording is the paper's own loose phrasing (an apparent conflation of "messages" and "operators"); the body text is unambiguous and should be treated as authoritative. Table III separately reports per-operator error rates for a 12-operator subset for whom English, letter-cipher, and number-cipher texts were all available [fact, p. 22].

**No SNR or radio-noise condition is stated for this test at all.** The only noise-related sentence in the paper is speculative, not a description of the test setup: Gold attributes the "harder to correct" error categories (types 7–13, e.g., extra/missing dots or spaces) to "operator sending lapses and also ... noisy radio reception, e.g., fading, atmospheric noise, adjacent channel interference," as *possible* real-world causes in general use — not as a controlled condition of the 184-message test [fact, p. 22]. There is no mention anywhere in the paper of tape recordings, an RF/audio front end, or a stated SNR; combined with the absence of any receiver/detector stage in Fig. 5, the most defensible reading is that the test corpus was captured from a clean keying source (hand key or bug, directly, per §II) with no radio channel involved [inference].

**How the headline number was actually produced** (§IX, pp. 22–23; Tables I and IV):
1. Gold picks a **crude, explicitly-labeled-as-crude acceptability threshold**: a message is "acceptable" if it has under 6% character errors, based on unpublished Van Hoosen data (footnote 6, p. 23: "the best guess based on Van Hoosen's data") about how easily humans tolerate 1–2% text error.
2. Table I (p. 22), using the **original, single global fixed-Δ design** (Δ's picked by hand from ~10 of the 184 messages, then frozen for all 184): at the 6% row, N = 154 of 184 messages acceptable (30 unacceptable) → **83.7%**.
3. Table IV (p. 23) reruns the comparison counting only error types 1)–6) (the "close threshold decision" errors that Δ can affect) under two refined schemes: **Fixed Δ** (chosen per operator, then held fixed for that operator's message — §V) gives 164/184 acceptable at 6% → **89.1%**; **Variable Δ** (recomputed every 100 samples, IBM 704 simulation) gives 177/184 → **96.2%**.
4. **89% to 96% (Fixed-Δ vs. Variable-Δ, both at the 6%-character-error acceptability line) is the actual pair of numbers behind the abstract's rounded "90 per cent and 95 per cent."** Full Table I, II, III, IV values are reproduced below for reference.

Table I (character errors, N of 184 messages under the given %, original fixed Δ): 0.25%→43, 0.5%→58, 0.75%→76, 1%→87, 1.5%→111, 2%→127, 3%→139, 4%→145, 5%→149, 6%→154, 7%→157, 8%→164, 9%→166, 10%→169, over 10%→184 [fact, p. 22].

Table II (space errors — a letter space mistaken for a word space or vice versa, N of 184): 1%→5, 2%→18, 3%→34, 4%→53, 5%→71, 10%→109, 15%→123, 20%→124 [fact, p. 22].

Table IV (character errors, types 1–6 only, Fixed Δ vs. Variable Δ; plus a third column of type 7–12 errors that Δ cannot affect): at 6%, Fixed Δ = 164, Variable Δ = 177, "incorrect number of marks" (types 7–12) column = 178 [fact, p. 23].

### 2.5 Error taxonomy and limitations (§VIII, pp. 21–22; §IX, p. 23)

Gold gives 13 named error types (examples in the paper): (1) L6 failure, (2) alphabet-test failure, (3) two characters run together, (4) split characters, (5) simple mark errors, (6) complex mark errors, (7) extra dot (simple), (8) extra dot (complex, mostly in numbers), (9) missing dot (simple), (10) missing dot (complex), (11) extra space, (12) missing space, (13) complex/unattributable errors (~10% of analyzed garbles, e.g., THE→XT, JUMP→WXTY) [fact, pp. 21–22]. About half of all errors were types 1–6 (threshold/rule failures); the rest reflect operator lapses or (speculatively) channel noise [fact, p. 22].

Explicit, stated limitations:
- **The alphabet test cannot resolve genuine ambiguity in the Morse alphabet itself**: it successfully separates O (- - -) from S (...) but cannot separate T from U-run-together, because X (dash-dot-dot-dot = "TU" concatenated) is itself a valid Morse character; Gold states this "could not be rectified on a letter basis, but would require more complicated contextual rules" [fact, p. 21].
- **Error types 7–13 have no known fix within MAUDE's rule framework**: "No way is known to correct these errors without the use of more complex contextual rules involving knowledge of word structure" [fact, p. 23].
- Even granting a hypothetical fix for *all* types 1–6, Gold concludes "there would still be a significant difference in performance between a man and MAUDE" [fact, p. 23] — i.e., the rule-based design has a hard ceiling below human performance.
- Sender-to-sender variability in run-together vs. split-character tendency is noted directly from the two worked examples in Fig. 6: one operator tended to run letters together (more L6/alphabet-test failures), the other was decoded perfectly and is called "fairly typical for about half of the operators" [fact, p. 21] — i.e., roughly half the 53-operator population decoded essentially error-free under this scheme, and the aggregate 83–96% acceptable-message figures are pulled down by a harder-to-decode subset.
- The α = 0.18 bug-correction is explicitly reported as *not* generalizing well to bug-sent code (§V, p. 19), a stated per-sending-method limitation.

### 2.6 The Appendix

A statistical theorem (credited to a generalization of a result by L. C. Maximon, an unpublished 1956 Lincoln Lab report) underlies the fixed rules L6, S6, M6: for a run of k independent order statistics with probability q of being classified "short," it derives the probability that the m-th term is correctly picked out as the extreme value of its window; Table V tabulates this probability for k = 3, 4, 5 [fact, pp. 23–24]. Gold references a separate, fuller paper on this theory as "submitted for publication" [fact, p. 24] — not the same as the two companion papers mentioned in the Introduction. **[uncertain]** I did not find this separate theory paper's citation in the reference list here (Gold cites only Eisenstadt, Gold, et al., internal Group Report 34-57, 1957, and Maximon's Group Report 34-53, 1956, both marked "not generally available").

## 4. Effect on ranking / benchmark plan

**[inference]** No change to the project's decoder ranking (dit-matched filter → CNN+LSTM+CTC → Bell-style HMM → hybrids). Gold/MAUDE remains correctly placed as the "adaptive-threshold duration classifier" baseline: it operates entirely on already-extracted element durations, never touches signal-in-noise, and its own best reported number (96.2% "acceptable" messages by a crude 6%-character-error criterion) is under conditions with no receiver noise at all and a generous pass/fail bar — not a like-for-like comparison with the project's ±150 Hz, 1500 sample/s weak-signal setting. If anything, the newly-read two-stage design (structural order-statistics rules before any threshold) is a mildly useful precedent for the project's own "use Morse/callsign structure to constrain decisions before falling back to soft/likelihood-based classification" approach, but it does not change the benchmark plan.
